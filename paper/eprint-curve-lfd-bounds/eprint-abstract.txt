We study the last fall degree (LFD) of the summation-polynomial systems of elliptic-curve index calculus, for prime-field curves with a subgroup factor base of order $2^k$ written with squaring chains, and for binary and Koblitz curves with Boolean descents. For two summands on prime-field curves we prove $\mathrm{LFD}\le k+2$ at every depth $k$. We also prove that the LFD of unit-ideal instances with target abscissa $t\ne0$ is unbounded in $k$: in characteristic $0$ or $p>2^{k+1}$ it is at least $\lfloor(k-10)/14\rfloor+1$ for $k\ge38$. The upper bound is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib, as are the bounds $\mathrm{LFD}\le\max(mk+1,\delta)$ for $m$ summands and inputs of degree at most $\delta$, $\mathrm{LFD}\ge4$ for two summands with unit ideal and $\mathrm{LFD}\ge7$ for three summands at $k\ge3$, under explicit conditions on the target. The growth bound has a written proof. Lean checks it for a target of order two on the curves $y^2=x^3+ax-(1+a)$, for all but four rational $a$, over $\mathbb{C}$ and over prime fields above an explicit cutoff; for other targets Lean reduces it to a vanishing-sum bound in the function field of the curve. Exact computations with certificates give, for two summands and generic targets, $\mathrm{LFD}=4$ for $k\le5$, $5$ for $k=6,\ldots,9$ and $6$ for $k=10,\ldots,13$ on a unit instance. These values match the corrected degree-of-regularity breakpoints of Petit, Kosters and Messeng. At $k=14$ we certify $\mathrm{LFD}\ge7$; the matching upper bound comes from a degree lift that has a written proof but no final certificate. We conjecture $\mathrm{LFD}(k)=\lfloor(k+14)/4\rfloor$. We also report a certified sweep of 3005 systems, exact values for three to eight summands, where the bound $mk+1$ is attained at depth $k\le m-1$ on $409$ of $412$ instances, and exact values for binary and Koblitz systems through dimension $16$. By a theorem of Caminata and Gorla these results bound the solving degree: it is at most $k+2$ for two summands on prime-field curves, and it equals the LFD on all but eight of the 5813 systems whose LFD we determine. We do not estimate discrete-logarithm costs.
